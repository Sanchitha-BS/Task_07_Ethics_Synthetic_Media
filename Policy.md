# Phase B: Governance Policy

## Organizational Context

This policy is written for the Learning & Development (L&D) function within a large enterprise software company, modeled on the kind of organization I worked in as a Solution Architect at Oracle Cerner. This team produces employee-facing training content, onboarding materials, and periodic internal communications for a global, multi-office workforce.

Three recurring content types create real temptation to use synthetic media, and anchor this policy:

1. **Multi-language training narration** — dubbing existing, already-approved training videos into other languages using synthetic voices, instead of re-filming with a translator or hiring voice talent for each language and each update.
2. **Exit interview and performance-review summary narration** — a synthetic voice reading back a written summary to an employee, raising the question of whether sensitive, personal HR content should ever be delivered by a synthetic voice at all.
3. **Internal "leadership update" podcast series** — a recurring, synthetic-narrated internal audio update summarizing company news, distinct from an executive's own recorded voice, but still capable of being mistaken for an official leadership statement if not clearly branded.

Alongside these, the L&D team also produces new-hire welcome videos and occasionally substitutes a synthetic executive message for monthly or bi-yearly town-hall "connects" when the real executive cannot attend. The audience for all of this is the company's own workforce, from new hires with no context for the company's internal norms to tenured employees who would recognize a real executive's voice and manner. Employees make real decisions, compliance behavior, trust in leadership, understanding of their own HR outcomes, based on this content, which is why this policy exists.

## Policy: Use of Synthetic Media in Employee Communications and Training

**Applies to:** All Learning & Development staff, internal communications staff, and any vendor or contractor producing content on the company's behalf.

### 1. Permitted Uses

The following are permitted, subject to the disclosure and provenance requirements in Sections 4 and 5:

- **Multi-language localization of already-approved training content.** A training video that has already gone through standard review in its original language may be dubbed into additional languages using a synthetic voice, provided the underlying content is unchanged and the original presenter (if a real person) has consented to their content being localized this way (see Section 3).
- **Fully synthetic, non-human presenters for procedural content.** Software walkthroughs, system demos, and process explainers may use a synthetic voice or animated avatar that does not represent any real, identifiable person, from the start.
- **Accessibility narration.** Converting written policies, SOPs, or announcements into audio format for employees who need or prefer audio.

### 2. Prohibited Uses

The following are refused outright, regardless of intent, disclosure, or business justification:

- **Synthetic delivery of exit interview or performance-review summaries to employees.** This content is personal, consequential to the employee's standing and record, and must be delivered by the real person responsible for it, in their own voice. No disclosure or quality standard makes synthetic delivery of this category acceptable.
- **Synthetic executive response to a live crisis, controversy, layoff, or safety incident.** When leadership communication matters most for trust, it must come from the real person, live or genuinely recorded, not a synthetic substitute produced because the executive is unavailable.
- **Reuse of a departed or former employee's voice or likeness in any new content**, training or otherwise, without renewed, explicit consent obtained after their departure. Consent given while employed does not carry forward.
- **Any synthetic "employee testimonial"** for recruiting, culture marketing, or similar purposes that is composited, fabricated, or attributed to a person who did not actually say those words.
- **Cloning or approximating the voice or likeness of any real employee, manager, or executive without that specific person's documented consent for that specific use**, even internally, even for training roleplay scenarios (e.g., simulating a real past incident using a voice modeled closely on the actual people involved).

### 3. Consent Workflow

When a real, identifiable person's voice, likeness, or closely-modeled persona is used in synthetic media under this policy:

- **Who obtains consent:** The producing team's manager (L&D lead or Internal Communications lead) is responsible for obtaining and documenting consent before production begins. Consent cannot be assumed from an employee's general employment agreement.
- **What consent must specify:** the exact content and use case (e.g., "localization dubbing of the Q3 compliance training into Spanish, French, and Mandarin"), the expected distribution (internal only vs. any external use), and the expected duration of use.
- **Documentation:** Consent is recorded in writing (email or signed form) and stored with the project file for the content it covers. Verbal-only consent is not sufficient.
- **Validity period:** Consent is valid only for the specific use described and lapses automatically when the person leaves the company, when the underlying content is substantively changed, or after 24 months, whichever comes first. Renewal requires a new consent record.
- **Revocation:** Any employee may revoke consent for future use at any time by notifying their manager and L&D; existing distributed content is not retroactively recalled unless the person requests it and legal/HR agrees removal is warranted, but no new distribution or reuse may occur after revocation.

### 4. Disclosure Standards

Learning from Task 6: disclosure that lives only in a file name or a README does not travel with the content once it is shared, downloaded, or forwarded. Disclosure under this policy must live inside the content itself.

- Every piece of synthetic-voice or synthetic-avatar content must include a **spoken or on-screen disclosure at the start** ("This training narration was generated using AI voice technology" or equivalent), not only in accompanying documentation.
- The internal leadership-update podcast must state, at the start of every episode, that it is AI-narrated and is not the voice of any specific executive or leader.
- Localized/dubbed training must retain the disclosure in every language version, translated, not dropped.
- If content is excerpted, clipped, or re-shared internally (e.g., a short clip pulled for a team meeting), the excerpt must carry the same disclosure; it is the responsibility of whoever re-shares the content to preserve it.

### 5. Provenance Requirements

- All synthetic media produced under this policy must retain the generating tool's embedded content credentials (e.g., C2PA metadata) wherever the tool provides them, and must not be re-encoded or re-exported through any process known to strip that metadata without re-verification afterward.
- Before final publication, the producing team must run a C2PA/provenance check (as done in Task 6) and confirm the credential is intact, logging the result with the project file.
- Where the generation tool does not support embedded credentials, the producing team must maintain its own internal record: tool used, date generated, script/source content, and settings, retained for the life of the content.

### 6. Review and Approval

- Any content involving a real employee's likeness or voice, or any executive-facing content (welcome videos, town-hall substitutes), requires sign-off from both the L&D lead and HR before production begins.
- Fully synthetic, non-human-presenter content (system walkthroughs, procedural narration with no real-person likeness) requires only standard L&D content review, no additional HR sign-off.
- No content under this policy is published without a named individual confirming disclosure (Section 4) and provenance (Section 5) requirements were met.

### 7. Incident Response

If synthetic content produced under this policy is misused, misattributed, taken out of context, or reaches an unintended audience (e.g., an internal training clip is leaked externally and presented as a real, undisclosed recording):

- The employee or team who discovers the issue reports it immediately to L&D leadership and Internal Communications.
- L&D and Communications jointly assess scope (who received it, in what form) and issue a corrective notice to the original intended audience and, if externally leaked, prepare a public clarification in coordination with Legal and PR.
- The specific content is pulled from internal distribution pending review.
- Any case involving a real person's voice or likeness used or represented in a way they did not consent to is treated as an HR matter in addition to a communications one, and that person is notified directly, not left to learn about it secondhand.

### 8. Refusal

Beyond the outright prohibitions in Section 2, this team reserves the right to decline any synthetic-media request that:

- Involves an employee who has expressed discomfort with the use of their voice or likeness in any AI-generated content, even for a use that would otherwise be permitted.
- Would require reproducing sensitive, personal, or disciplinary content through a synthetic voice, regardless of the specific business justification offered.
- Cannot be adequately disclosed given its distribution format (e.g., a format with no practical way to attach a spoken or visible disclosure).

A request falling into refusal is documented, along with the reason, so the same request is not repeatedly re-raised without addressing the original concern.

## Limitations

This policy relies on people acting in good faith in several places, and that reliance has real limits.

**Where a well-meaning employee could get this wrong:** the localization use case is the easiest to misuse without realizing it. An employee dubbing an approved training video might reasonably assume that because the original content was approved, no fresh consent check is needed, when in fact the presenter's original consent may not have covered synthetic dubbing at all if that wasn't specified when it was first obtained. This policy assumes consent records are specific enough to catch that gap, but older content produced before this policy existed almost certainly lacks that specificity, and someone would have to notice the gap rather than just proceeding.

**Where a bad-faith actor defeats this policy entirely:** nothing in this policy stops an employee or manager outside the L&D/Communications workflow from generating unauthorized synthetic content on a personal account, using a free tool exactly as I did in Task 6, and distributing it informally (a Slack message, a personal chat) rather than through any reviewed channel. This policy governs official production, not what any individual with a laptop and an internet connection can quietly do on their own, which, as my Task 6 experience showed, requires no special skill or access.

**Where this policy admits it cannot guarantee an outcome:** the disclosure requirement (Section 4) assumes the disclosure travels with the content. My own Task 6 finding was that a README-level disclosure doesn't survive a file being copied out of its original context; and even an in-content spoken disclosure does not survive if someone deliberately clips it out before re-sharing. This policy raises the bar (disclosure inside the content, not just around it) but cannot make disclosure literally unremovable.

**Where good faith is inescapable rather than a weakness:** the consent workflow depends on managers actually documenting consent rather than treating it as a formality, and on employees actually reading and understanding what they're consenting to. No paperwork requirement forces genuine understanding. I consider this an honest limitation rather than a fatal one: any policy governing human behavior ultimately depends on people applying it in good faith, and the alternative, assuming universal bad faith, would make the policy so restrictive it couldn't function for its legitimate, lower-stakes uses (like accessibility narration or system-walkthrough videos).

**What this policy does not attempt to solve:** it does not stop a determined, resourced adversary (external or internal) from producing convincing unauthorized synthetic content entirely outside company systems, and it does not guarantee detection of a violation before harm occurs, only a process for responding once one is discovered. Those are the same gaps my Task 6 mitigation survey found in disclosure, provenance, and detection generally: this policy narrows the company's own exposure, it does not close the underlying gap in the technology.
